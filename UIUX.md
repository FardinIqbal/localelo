1: The Core Philosophy: Don't Make Me Think
2: Krug's First Law of Usability: "Don't Make Me Think!"
Krug's First Law of Usability is the single most important principle and the ultimate tie-breaker when designing a website.
The law is, simply: "Don't make me think!".
This principle means that as far as humanly possible, a web page should be obvious and self-evident. When a user looks at a page, they should be able to "get it"—what it is, and how to use it—without expending any effort thinking about it.
3: The User's Mental State: Eliminating Question Marks
Your primary job as a designer is to get rid of the question marks in the user's head.
A "Not Thinking" (Good) Experience: When a page doesn't make a user think, their internal monologue is simple and declarative.
"OK. This looks like the product categories...".
"There it is: Monitors. Click.".
A "Thinking" (Bad) Experience: When a page makes a user think, their thought balloons are filled with question marks.
"Is that the navigation? Or is that it over there?".
"Hmm. Why did they call it that?".
"Can I click on that?".
"Those two links seem like the same thing. Are they really?" .
3: The Goal: Self-Evident vs. Self-Explanatory Design
The Ideal: Self-Evident. Your goal should be for each page to be self-evident, meaning the average user will know what it is and how to use it just by looking at it.
The Compromise: Self-Explanatory. Sometimes, especially for complex or groundbreaking sites, you have to settle for self-explanatory. On a self-explanatory page, it takes a little thought to "get it," but all the visual cues—well-chosen names, layout, and crafted text—work together to create near-instantaneous recognition.
The Rule: If you can't make a page self-evident, you at least need to make it self-explanatory.
3: Common "Thinking" Triggers to Eliminate
Question marks are generated by anything that is ambiguous or requires cognitive effort.
4: Ambiguous Naming
Typical culprits for bad names are cute/clever names, marketing-induced names, company-specific names, and unfamiliar technical names.
Example: Job Listings
Obvious: Jobs. The user thinks, "Jobs! Click.".
Requires Milliseconds of Thought: Employment Opportunities. The user thinks, "Hmm. [Milliseconds of thought] Jobs. Click." .
Requires Thought: Job-o-Rama. The user thinks, "Hmm. Could be Jobs. But it sounds like more than that. Should I click or keep looking?".
While there are always tradeoffs (e.g., "Jobs" may sound undignified, or politics may lock in "Job-o-Rama"), the tradeoffs should always be skewed further in the direction of "Obvious" than we tend to think.
4: Ambiguous Clickability
A user should never have to devote a millisecond of thought to whether things are clickable or not.
Example: A "Results" Button
Obvious: A button with a clear 3D border.
Requires Thought: A "flat" button with no border.
Requires Thought: Text with a small arrow next to it that doesn't look like a standard link or button.
The argument "they can just move the cursor to see if it changes" is flawed. Every question mark, no matter how small, adds to the user's cognitive workload, distracts them, and erodes their confidence in the site.
4: Making the User Think About Mechanics
You should never make the user think about how the site's internal mechanics work.
Example: Bookstore Search
The Problem: Most sites forced users to choose how to search before searching (e.g., "Search by: Title, Author, Keyword"). This creates "mental chatter" like, "All I know is it's by Tom Clancy. Is Clancy a keyword? What is a keyword, anyway?".
The Solution (Amazon): Amazon.com was a pioneer in not making the user think about this distinction. They just provide one search box. The user types "Tom Clancy," and the system is smart enough to figure out they meant an author.
The Principle: Why should the user have to think about how the site's search engine wants them to phrase the question?.
2: Why the First Law is So Important
The main reason "Don't Make Me Think" is so crucial is not the one people usually cite.
The Common Myth: "The competition is always just one click away".
The Reality: This is only sometimes true. Many people will tough it out. They tend to blame themselves for problems, not the site. Furthermore, finding the site may have been hard, and the prospect of starting over isn't attractive.
The Real Reason: The main reason "Don't Make Me Think" is critical is that most people are going to spend far less time looking at our pages than we'd like to think.
If pages are to be effective, they have to work their magic at a glance.
Puzzling over unimportant things saps our energy, time, and enthusiasm.
Using a site that doesn't make us think just feels effortless and makes everything seem better, like good lighting in a store.

1: How We Really Use the Web: The 3 Facts of Life
To design effective websites, you must accept three facts about how users actually behave, which is often contrary to how we think they behave.
When we create sites, we act as though people will pore over each page, read our finely crafted text, and weigh their options.
The reality is much different. The user's reality is not "great literature"; it's a "billboard going by at 60 miles an hour".
2: Fact #1: We Don't Read Pages. We Scan Them.
One of the few well-documented facts about Web use is that people spend very little time reading most pages. Instead, we scan (or skim) them, looking for words or phrases that catch our eye. The exception is pages with documents like news stories, but even then, if it's long, we're likely to print it.
3: Why We Scan
We're in a hurry. Much of our Web use is motivated by the desire to save time. Web users act like sharks: they have to keep moving, or they'll die.
We know we don't need to read everything. We are only interested in a fraction of what's on the page—the bits that match our task.
We're good at it. We've been scanning newspapers and magazines all our lives to find the parts we're interested in.
3: The "Ginger" Analogy
The effect is like the Far Side cartoon about what we say to dogs versus what they hear. The owner says a long-winded speech, but all the dog (Ginger) hears is "blah blah GINGER blah blah blah GINGER".
Like Ginger, users scan pages and tend to only focus on trigger words that match: a) The task at hand. b) Personal interests. c) Hardwired words like "Free," "Sale," or our own name.
2: Fact #2: We Don't Make Optimal Choices. We Satisfice.
When designing, we tend to assume users will scan the page, consider all available options, and choose the best one.
In reality, most of the time we satisfice (a term coined by Herbert Simon, mixing "satisfying" and "sufficing"). We choose the first reasonable option that we find, not the best one. As soon as we find a link that seems like it might lead to what we want, we'll click it.
3: The Firefighter Analogy (Naturalistic Decision Making)
Research by Gary Klein on high-stakes decision-makers (like firefighters) found they didn't follow the rational model of comparing options.
Fire commanders at a fire scene did not compare any options. They took the first reasonable plan that came to mind and did a quick mental test for problems. If they didn't find any, that was their plan.
3: Why Web Users Satisfice
We're in a hurry. Optimizing is hard and takes a long time. Satisficing is more efficient.
There's not much of a penalty for guessing wrong. Unlike firefighting, the penalty on a website is just a click or two of the Back button. The Back button is the most-used feature of Web browsers.
Weighing options may not improve our chances. On poorly designed sites, putting in the effort doesn't help. You're better off just guessing.
Guessing is more fun. It's less work, and it introduces an element of chance and discovery.
2: Fact #3: We Don't Figure Out How Things Work. We Muddle Through.
Usability testing quickly reveals the extent to which people use things all the time without understanding how they work, or with completely wrong-headed ideas about them.
Very few people read instructions. Instead, we forge ahead and muddle through, making up our own vaguely plausible stories about what we're doing and why it works.
3: The "Great Seal of England" Analogy
This behavior is like the scene in The Prince and the Pauper where the pauper, left alone, uses the Great Seal of England as a nutcracker. To him, it was just a big, heavy chunk of metal that worked for the job.
3: Web-Based Examples of Muddling
This is not limited to beginners; even savvy users have gaps in their understanding.
Example: People who type a site's entire URL (e.g., www.ebay.com) into the Yahoo search box every single time they want to go there.
The Flawed Mental Model: If you ask them, it's clear some of them think that Yahoo is the Internet, and that this is the way you use it.
3: Why We Muddle
It's not important to us. It's not for lack of intelligence, but for lack of caring. As long as we can use it, we don't care how it works.
If we find something that works, we stick to it. Once we find a way—no matter how badly—we tend not to look for a better way.
3: Why "Getting It" Matters
Muddling through may work sometimes, but it's inefficient and error-prone.
If users "get it" (understand the site):
There's a much better chance they'll find what they're looking for.
They'll understand the full range of what your site offers, not just the parts they stumble across.
You have a better chance of steering them to the parts you want them to see.
They'll feel smarter and more in control, which will bring them back.
You can get away with a site people muddle through only until a competitor builds one that makes them feel smart.
2: The Grand Strategy: Design for Scanning
The conclusion from these three facts is simple: If your audience is going to act like you're designing billboards, then design great billboards.

1: Krug's Other Laws: Optimizing Clicks and Words
Beyond the First Law, Krug provides two other "laws" that stem from the core philosophy.
2: Krug's Second Law of Usability: Mindless Clicks
The old debate about how many clicks is "too many" (e.g., the "three-click rule") is a distraction.
Krug's Second Law: "It doesn't matter how many times I have to click, as long as each click is a mindless, unambiguous choice".
What really counts is not the number of clicks, but the difficulty of each click—the amount of thought required and the amount of uncertainty. Users don't mind a lot of clicks if they are painless and they have continued confidence that they're on the right track (following the "scent of information").
A "Mindless" Choice (Good): The first question in the game Twenty Questions: "Animal, vegetable, or mineral?". As long as you accept the premise, it requires no thought.
A "Thoughtful" Choice (Bad): A site asking a user with a home office to choose between Home and Office. This is ambiguous and causes hesitation.
A "Thoughtful" Choice (Bad): A Symantec update page that lists the product as "NAV for Windows 95/98". This forces the user to think, "Is 'NAV' the same as 'Norton AntiVirus'? I'm on Windows 98... is that the same as 'Windows 95/98'?".
2: Krug's Third Law of Usability: Omit Needless Words
This law is inspired by E. B. White's rule in The Elements of Style: "Omit needless words".
Krug's Third Law: "Get rid of half the words on each page, then get rid of half of what's left".
This sounds excessive, but it's meant to encourage ruthlessness. Most words on a web page are just taking up space, and no one is going to read them. By being there, they suggest they might be important, making the page seem more daunting than it is.
3: Benefits of Omitting Words
It reduces the noise level of the page.
It makes the useful content more prominent.
It makes the pages shorter, allowing users to see more at a glance without scrolling.
3: The Two Main Culprits to Eliminate
4: 1. Happy Talk
What it is: The introductory text that's supposed to welcome us to the site, tell us how great it is, or explain what we're about to see.
The Sure-Fire Test: If you listen closely while reading it, you can hear a tiny voice in your head saying, "Blah blah blah blah blah...".
Why it's bad: It's content-free, like small talk. Web users don't have time for small talk; they want to get right to the beef.
Where it lives: Often on "section front" pages, used as filler.
4: 2. Instructions
The Problem: The main thing you need to know about instructions is that no one is going to read them.
User Behavior: Users will try "muddling through" first, and only read instructions as a last resort after repeated failures.
The Goal: Your objective should always be to eliminate instructions entirely by making everything self-explanatory.
The Compromise: When instructions are absolutely necessary, cut them back to the bare minimum.
Example: The Verizon site survey page.
Before: 103 words of happy talk ("The following questionnaire is designed to...") and instructions on how to use drop-down menus.
After (Pruned): 41 words that get straight to the point ("Please help us improve the site... It should only take you 2-3 minutes...") .

1: Designing for Scanning: The "Billboard" Approach
Given that users scan, satisfice, and muddle, our design strategy must adapt. We must design our pages as "billboards". There are five key things we can do to ensure users see and understand as much as possible.
2: Principle 1: Create a Clear Visual Hierarchy
This is one of the best ways to make a page easy to grasp. The page's appearance—all its visual cues—must clearly and accurately portray the relationships between the things on the page.
It's like preprocessing the page for the user, organizing and prioritizing its contents so they can grasp it almost instantly.
3: The Three Traits of a Clear Hierarchy
Prominence: The more important something is, the more prominent it is. This is achieved through size (larger), formatting (bolder), distinctive color, white space, or position (nearer the top).
Grouping: Things that are logically related are also visually related. This is done by grouping them together under a heading, in a clearly defined area, or by giving them a similar visual style.
Nesting: Things are "nested" visually to show what's part of what. For example, a section ("Computer Books") should visually span all the elements of the book ("One Particular Computer Book," its description, its price) because the book is part of that section .
3: The Newspaper Analogy
We parse visual hierarchies all the time. A newspaper page uses them to give us information before we read a word.
The biggest headline is the most important story.
A headline spanning three columns tells us those three columns are one story.
3: The Cost of a Flawed Hierarchy
When a page has no clear hierarchy (e.g., everything looks equally important), the user is forced to do the work of scanning and trying to form their own sense of what's important. This is a lot more work.
A flawed hierarchy is like reading a carelessly constructed sentence: "Bill put the cat on the table for a minute because it was a little wobbly.". We can figure it out, but it throws us momentarily and forces us to think when we shouldn't have to.
2: Principle 2: Take Advantage of Conventions
Conventions are the publishing equivalent of "Don't Make Me Think." They are established, widely used design patterns, like the shopping cart icon , page numbers, or headlines.
3: Why Conventions Are Your Friends
They work. Conventions only become conventions if they are effective.
They reduce the learning curve. They make it easier for users to go from site to site without expending effort figuring out how things work.
3: The Designer's Dilemma
Designers are often reluctant to use conventions.
Why? They feel (not incorrectly) that they've been hired to do something new and different, not "the same old thing".
The Reality: Praise, awards, and high-profile job offers are rarely based on criteria like "best use of conventions".
3: The "Reinventing the Wheel" Framework
Sometimes reinventing the wheel results in a revolutionary device. Sometimes it's just... reinventing the wheel.
The Rule for Innovation: Innovate only when you know you have a better idea (and everyone you show it to says "Wow!").
The Rule for Everything Else: Take advantage of conventions when you don't.
The If/Then Logic: If you are not going to use an existing convention, you must be sure your replacement is either:
(a) So clear and self-explanatory that there's no learning curve (it's as good as a convention), OR
(b) It adds so much value that it's worth a small learning curve.
2: Principle 3: Break Pages into Clearly Defined Areas
This is critically important for scanning. Dividing the page into clearly defined areas allows users to quickly decide which areas of the page to focus on and which areas they can safely ignore.
The $25,000 Pyramid Analogy: A user should be able to glance at a well-designed page and play the game: "Things I can do on this site!" "Links to today's top stories!" "Navigation to get to the rest of the site!" .
The Eye-Tracking Evidence: Studies show that users decide very quickly (in the first few seconds) which parts of a page are likely to have useful information, and then almost never look at the other parts.
2: Principle 4: Make it Obvious What's Clickable
This is a core component of "Don't Make Me Think". Since users are scanning for the next thing to click, it's vital to make the clickable items obvious.
Bad Example: Senator Orrin Hatch's 2000 presidential site. It had 18 links, but all the text on the page was in color, so there was no way to distinguish the links from the non-links at a glance.
The Cost: Forcing users to think about something that should be mindless squanders their limited reservoir of patience and goodwill.
2: Principle 5: Keep the Noise Down to a Dull Roar
One of the great enemies of easy-to-grasp pages is visual noise.
3: Two Types of Noise
Busy-ness: When everything on the page is clamoring for your attention. This is the "Publisher's Clearing House" effect—lots of exclamation points, bright colors, and shouting .
Background Noise: This is like being at a cocktail party. No one source is too loud, but there are many tiny bits of visual noise that wear us down.
Example: The menus on MSNBC. The dividing lines between menu items add a lot of noise. Simply graying them out would make the menus much easier to scan .
3: The Designer's Blind Spot
The Rule of Thumb: When designing, it's a good idea to assume that everything is visual noise until proven otherwise.
The Designer vs. User Conflict: Designers love subtle cues because subtlety is a trait of sophisticated design. But Web users are in such a hurry that they routinely miss subtle cues.
The Designer's Self-Correction: In general, if you're a designer and you think a visual cue is sticking out like a sore thumb, it probably means you need to make it twice as prominent.

1: Application: Designing Usable Navigation
Navigation is the most critical application of these principles. People won't use your site if they can't find their way around it.
2: The Core Problem: The Unbearable Lightness of Browsing
Navigation isn't just a feature of a Web site; in many ways, it is the Web site. Without it, there's no "there" there.
We talk about "Web navigation" but not "department store navigation" because "figuring out where you are" is a much more pervasive problem on the Web. We are inherently lost on the Web.
3: How the Web Lacks Physical Cues
When we move around in the physical world, we build a mental map. The Web experience is missing these cues.
No sense of scale. We have very little sense of how big a site is (50 pages or 17,000?). This makes it hard to know if you've seen everything, or when to stop looking.
No sense of direction. There is no "left" and "right," no "up" and "down".
No sense of location. In a store, we can take a shortcut ("Chainsaws are in the back, near the refrigerators"). On the Web, we "teleport" by clicking links. To return, we must remember where it is in the conceptual hierarchy and retrace our steps.
This is why bookmarks and the Back button are so important.
It's also why the Home page is crucial; it's the fixed "North Star" of the site.
3: The Overlooked Purposes of Navigation
Navigation does more than just help us find things and tell us where we are.
It gives us something to hold on to. Good navigation creates virtual "ground" under our feet and "handrails" to hold, making us feel grounded.
It tells us what's here. This is a critical, overlooked function. Navigation reveals the site's content and hierarchy.
It tells us how to use the site. Done correctly, the navigation should be all the instructions a user needs.
It gives us confidence in the people who built it. Clear, well-thought-out navigation is one of the best opportunities a site has to create a good impression.
2: The "Trunk Test": A Framework for Good Navigation
This is the acid test for good web navigation.
The Test: Imagine you've been blindfolded, locked in the trunk of a car, driven around, and dumped on a page deep in a Web site.
The Goal: When your vision clears, if the page is well designed, you should be able to answer 6 key questions without hesitation.
Why this test? This is not an abstract exercise. Users are often dropped in the middle of a site with no idea where they are, because they followed a link from a search engine or another site .
3: The 6 Questions You Must Be Able to Answer Instantly
What site is this? (Site ID)
What page am I on? (Page Name)
What are the major sections of this site? (Sections)
What are my options at this level? (Local Navigation)
Where am I in the scheme of things? ("You are here" Indicators)
How can I search? (Search)

2: Designing the Components to Pass the Trunk Test
Passing the "Trunk Test" requires a system of navigation components, starting with the navigation that follows you everywhere.
3: Component 1: Persistent (Global) Navigation
What it is: The set of navigation elements that appear on every page of a site.
What it does: It provides a calm, comforting voice that says, "The navigation is over here... it will always be here, and it will always work the same way".
The Benefits:
Gives instant confirmation that you're still in the same site.
Means you only have to figure out how it works once.
4: The 5 Elements of Persistent Navigation
Persistent navigation should include the five elements you most need on hand at all times:
Site ID
Sections (Primary navigation)
Utilities (Help, Site Map, Shopping Cart, etc.)
A way Home
A way to Search
4: Exceptions: When Not to Use Persistent Navigation
There are two exceptions to the "every page" rule:
The Home Page: The Home page is not like other pages; it has different burdens to bear. This sometimes means it makes sense not to use the standard persistent navigation there.
Forms: On pages where a form needs to be filled in (like checkout, registration, or a survey), the persistent navigation can be an unnecessary distraction. For these pages, it's best to use a minimal version with just the Site ID, a link to Home, and any Utilities that might help fill out the form.
3: Component 2: The Site ID (Answering "What site is this?")
What it is: The "building name" for the website. On the Web, where travel is "teleportation," you need to see it on every page.
Location: At the top of the page, in or near the upper-left corner (for left-to-right languages).
Why: The Site ID represents the whole site, which is the highest level in the logical hierarchy. Putting it at the top visually "frames" everything else on the page.
Appearance: It needs to look like a Site ID—a distinctive typeface and a graphic that's recognizable as a logo.
3: Component 3: The Sections & Local Navigation (Answering "What's here?" and "What are my options?")
The Sections (Primary Navigation): These are the links to the main sections of the site, the top level of the hierarchy.
The Subsections (Secondary Navigation): The persistent navigation will also usually include space to display the list of subsections in the current section.
The Common Failing: The most common problem in Web design is failing to give the lower-level navigation (tertiary and beyond) the same attention as the top. In so many sites, the navigation breaks down and becomes ad-hoc as soon as you get past the second level.
The Cause: It's hard to design, designers run out of time, and people responsible for content haven't thought that far ahead .
The Moral: It's vital to have sample pages that show navigation for all potential levels before you start arguing about the Home page color scheme.
3: Component 4: The Utilities
What they are: Links to important elements of the site that are not part of the content hierarchy.
Examples: "Help," "Site Map," "Shopping Cart," "About Us," "Contact Us" .
Appearance: They should be less prominent than the Sections, similar to how a "Restrooms" sign is less prominent than a "Men's Shoes" department sign .
Limit: The persistent navigation can only accommodate 4 or 5 Utilities. The less-used leftovers can be grouped together on the Home page .
3: Component 5: A Way Home
Why it's crucial: Having a "Home" button in sight at all times offers reassurance. It's a "Reset" button or a "Get out of Jail free" card.
The Convention: There is an emerging convention that the Site ID also doubles as a button that takes you to the Home page.
The Problem: A surprising number of users still aren't aware of this convention.
The Solution (for now): It's a good idea to either:
Include an explicit "Home" page link in the Sections or Utilities, OR
Add the word "Home" discreetly to the Site ID graphic (everywhere except the Home page) to let people know it's clickable .
3: Component 6: Search (Answering "How can I search?")
Given how many users are "search-dominant," every page should have a search box (or a link to one, if the site is tiny).
4: The Simple, Unbreakable Search Formula
A large percentage of users, upon reaching a new site, will scan the page for one thing: a box, a button, and the word "Search" . Stick to this formula.
DON'T use fancy wording. They're looking for "Search," not "Find," "Quick Find," or "Keyword Search". (The only exception is using "Go" as the button label if "Search" is the label for the box) .
DON'T add instructions. "Type a keyword" is the new "Leave a message at the beep." It was once necessary, but now it just makes you sound clueless.
DON'T add options. Think very carefully before adding options to the persistent search box, such as scoping the search ("search just this section"). This makes the user think ("What are these options? Do I need them?").
The If/Then for Options: If you want to give users the option to scope a search, give it to them on the search results page, when it's actually useful (i.e., after their first, site-wide search returned too many results).
Make sure it works. If you do provide options (like "Search All" vs. "Search Quotes"), make sure they don't conflict. On IMDB, searching "All" for "badges" failed, but searching "Quotes" for "badges" succeeded. This destroys user confidence .
3: Component 7: Page Names (Answering "What page am I on?")
Page names are the "street signs" of the Web.
The Analogy: Driving in Los Angeles is wonderful because the street signs are big, well-placed, and legible from a distance. This lets you focus on traffic (the task) instead of navigating . Driving in Boston, where signs are hidden or missing, is a nightmare.
The Lesson: Your page names should be like L.A. street signs.
4: The 4 Rules of Page Names
Every page needs a name. Just like every corner needs a street sign. (And no, just highlighting the link in the navigation is not good enough. You need a name, too) .
The name needs to be in the right place. In the visual hierarchy, the page name should "frame" the unique content of that page (i.e., be inside the persistent navigation, and at the top of the content area).
The name needs to be prominent. It should be the largest text on the page, clearly identifying itself as the for the entire page.
The name needs to match what I clicked. This is a crucial, implicit "social contract". If I click a link that says "Hot mashed potatoes," the page I land on must be named "Hot mashed potatoes".
The Cost of Failure: Every time a site violates this, I'm forced to think, "Why are those two things different?". It diminishes my trust in the site and the competence of its builders.
3: Component 8: "You Are Here" Indicators (Answering "Where am I?")
How they work: They show you where you are in the "scheme of things" by highlighting your current location in whatever navigation lists, menus, or bars appear on the page.
Methods: This can be done by putting a pointer next to the item, changing its text color, using bold text, or reversing the button color.
The Most Common Failing: They are too subtle.
The Designer Trap: Designers love subtlety, but users in a hurry miss it.
The Solution: Make them stand out. It's best to apply more than one visual distinction (e.g., a different color and bold text).
3: Component 9: Breadcrumbs (A Navigation Accessory)
Breadcrumbs (named after Hansel and Gretel) show you the path from the Home page to where you are.
Example: You are here: Home > Hobbies > Book Collecting > Welcome.
Breadcrumbs vs. "You Are Here": A "You are here" indicator (like a highlighted tab) shows you where you are in the overall hierarchy. Breadcrumbs just show you the path you took to get there.
The Trap: Breadcrumbs are not a good replacement for a solid, 2-level persistent navigation scheme. They don't reveal enough of the site; they only show you one path, like a view with blinders.
The Value: They are self-explanatory, don't take up much room, and provide a convenient way to back up one level or go Home. They are best used as an accessory for a large site with a deep hierarchy.
4: Breadcrumb Best Practices
Put them at the top. This literally marginalizes them, making it clear they are an accessory, not the main navigation.
Use > as the separator. Trial and error has shown this is the best, as it visually suggests forward motion down through the levels.
Use tiny type. This again reinforces their accessory status.
Boldface the last item. The last item is the name of the current page, and it should have prominence.
Don't use them instead of a page name. Some sites try to make the (bold) last item in the list be the page name. This doesn't work, as it fights our expectation that headings are flush left or centered.
3: A Special Case: Using Tabs for Navigation
Tabs are one of the very few cases where using a physical metaphor (like dividers in a binder) actually works in a user interface.
4: 4 Reasons to Love Tabs
They're self-evident. No one, no matter how "computer illiterate," looks at tabs and says, "Hmmm. I wonder what those do?".
They're hard to miss. Their distinctive shape makes them hard to overlook, unlike simple button bars.
They're slick. They add visual polish while also serving a useful function.
They suggest a physical space. This is their magic. The active tab creates the visual illusion that it has physically moved to the front, which is a very strong cue for our brains. This creates a powerful sense of "being in" a section.
4: How to Implement Tabs Correctly (The Amazon Model)
Amazon was a pioneer in using tabs correctly. To be effective, tabs must have these attributes:
They must be drawn correctly. The "pop" effect is crucial. The active tab must be a different color/shade and it must physically connect with the space below it. This is what creates the illusion of being in front.
Bad: No connection, no pop.
Better: Connected, but no contrast. Limited pop.
Best (Amazon): Connected and high-contrast. It "pops" to the front.
They should be color-coded (as a secondary cue). Amazon used a different, vivid color for each active section (e.g., Music, Books) and used a neutral beige for all inactive tabs. This high contrast works even for color-blind users.
Warning: Never rely on color as the only cue. 1 in 12 men are color-blind, and many more users just aren't very aware of color coding .
There must be a tab selected on every page. This includes the Home page. If no tab is selected on entry, you lose the impact.
Amazon first had "Books" as the default tab. As they grew, they intelligently created a "Welcome" tab just for the Home page, solving the problem of which section to default to.

1: Application: The Unique Challenges of the Home Page
Designing a Home page is a unique and difficult challenge.
2: The Core Conflict: The Home Page is Beyond Your Control
The Home page is a special case governed by competing interests.
The "Beat the Clock" Analogy: Designing a Home page is like the 1950s TV game show. A contestant would be given a simple-sounding stunt, but just as they were ready, the emcee would add, "Oh, there's just one more thing... you have to do it... blindfolded." Or "...under water.". This is the Home page: just when you think you've covered all the bases, there's always one... more... thing.
The "Tragedy of the Commons": The Home page is the "waterfront property" of the Web—the most desirable real estate. Everyone with a stake in the site wants a piece of it. Like a shared town pasture ("commons"), any shared resource will inevitably be destroyed by overuse.
The "Golden Goose" Analogy: The problem is that promoting things on the Home page works too well. This leads all stakeholders to think, "Why don't I have one?". The rewards (a huge gain in traffic) go to the individual section, while the costs (a loss in overall effectiveness as the page gets more cluttered) are shared by all.
The Solution: Preserving the Home page from promotional overload requires constant vigilance and the education of stakeholders.
3: The Many Burdens of the Home Page (What it Must Do)
The Home page is loaded with jobs it must perform, all at once:
Site Identity and Mission: Tell me what this site is and what it's for.
Site Hierarchy: Give an overview of what the site offers (content: "What can I find?") and its features (tasks: "What can I do here?").
Search: Must have a prominently displayed search box.
Teases: Like a magazine cover, it must entice you with hints of the "good stuff" inside. This includes:
Content promos: Newest, best, or most popular content (top stories, hot deals).
Feature promos: Invites to explore sections or try features like personalization or email newsletters.
Timely Content: It must have "signs of life" (updated content, a new press release) to signal that it's not moribund.
Deals: Space allocated for advertising, cross-promotion, and co-branding.
Shortcuts: Links to the most frequently requested pieces of content (e.g., software updates).
Registration: Links for new users to register and for old users to sign in.
Establish Credibility and Trust: For some visitors, this is the only chance you get to make a good impression.
Show me where to start.
2: The First Casualty: Conveying the Big Picture
Given everything the Home page has to do, designing one inevitably involves compromise. As the pressure mounts to squeeze in one more thing, some things get lost.
The one thing you can't afford to lose—and the thing that most often gets lost—is conveying the big picture. Almost every time someone hands Krug a Home page design, he can count on one thing: They haven't made it clear enough what the site is.
3: The Four Questions a Home Page Must Answer at a Glance
As quickly and clearly as possible, the Home page needs to answer the four questions a user has when they first enter a new site.
What is this?
What can I do here?
What do they have here?
Why should I be here—and not somewhere else?
If the user can "get it" in the first few seconds, they are much more likely to correctly interpret everything else, leading to a successful experience.
3: The Top 5 Plausible Excuses for Not Spelling it Out (and Why They're Wrong)
Excuse: "We don't need to. It's obvious."
Rebuttal: It's only obvious to you because you're involved in building it. It is not obvious to everybody.
Excuse: "After people see it once, they'll find it annoying."
Rebuttal: Very few people will avoid a site just because they see the same explanation every time.
Excuse: "Anybody who really needs our site will know what it is."
Rebuttal: This is tempting to think, but it's just not true.
Excuse: "That's what our advertising is for."
Rebuttal: Even if they saw the ad, will they remember exactly what it was by the time they get to the site?.
Excuse: "We'll just add a 'First time visitor?' link."
Rebuttal: This is a good idea, but it is no substitute for spelling out the big picture in plain sight. Most people won't click it until they've already tried and failed to figure it out on their own.
2: How to Get the Message Across
Everything on the Home page can contribute, but there are two places where users expect to find explicit statements of what the site is about.
3: The Two Most Important Tools
The Tagline: This is one of the most valuable bits of real estate. It's the space right next to the Site ID. When we see a phrase visually connected to the ID, we read it as a description of the whole site.
The Welcome Blurb: This is a terse description of the site, displayed in a prominent block that's visible without scrolling ("above the fold").
3: Guidelines for the Message
Use as much space as necessary... The temptation is to use no space, because you think everyone knows what the site is. But a site with a novel proposition (like Essential.com, which let users choose utility providers) needs to use a lot of space to "splain" itself.
...but don't use any more space than necessary. Keep it short. Don't feel compelled to mention every great feature; just list the most important ones (maximum four).
DON'T use a mission statement as a Welcome blurb. Nobody reads corporate mission statements that sound like a Miss America finalist ("XYZCorp offers world-class solutions in the burgeoning field of blah blah blah...").
Test it. This is one of the most important things to test. You can't trust your own judgment; you must show the Home page to people from outside your organization.
3: A Deep Dive: The Tagline
A tagline is a pithy phrase that characterizes the whole enterprise. It's the one place on the page where users most expect to find a concise statement of the site's purpose.
4: Good Taglines vs. Bad Taglines
Good taglines are clear and informative.
Example: https://www.google.com/search?q=ComputerUnderground.com: "World's largest archive of cheap codes and game info".
Bad taglines are vague.
Example: SONICNET: "THE ONLINE MUSIC NETWORK".
Good taglines are just long enough. Six to eight words seems to be the right length to convey a full thought.
Good taglines convey differentiation and a clear benefit.
Example: refdesk.com: "The single best source for facts on the net".
Bad taglines sound generic.
Example: https://www.google.com/search?q=netmarket.com: "Save Time. Save Money. Save Your Sanity." This is all good, but tells us nothing about the site.
Good taglines are personable, lively, and sometimes clever.
The "Clever" Trap: Clever is good only if the cleverness helps convey the benefit, not obscure it.
Example: BabyCenter.com's tagline is "Cradle and all." This is very clever, but it might give visitors the impression that the site is just about buying baby stuff, when in reality it's also a source of information and advice.
4: Taglines vs. Mottos
Don't confuse a tagline with a motto.
A motto expresses a guiding principle or goal ("To protect and to serve"). It's lofty and reassuring, but if I don't know what the thing is, a motto isn't going to tell me.
A tagline conveys a value proposition.
4: When Can You Go Without a Tagline?
Some sites can get by without a tagline, but it's a very small club.
Household Words: The handful of sites that have already achieved household word status (e.g., ebay, amazon.com, salon.com).
Strong Offline Brands: Sites that are very well known from their offline origins (e.g., Smithsonian, CNN.com).
Krug argues that, personally, even these sites would benefit from a tagline.
2: The Fifth Question: "Where do I start?"
Once I know what I'm looking at, there's one more important question the Home page has to answer for me: "Where do I start?".
After a quick look, I should be able to say with confidence:
"Here's where to start if I want to search."
"Here's where to start if I want to browse."
"Here's where to start if I want to sample their best stuff."
3: The "Obscured Entry Point" Problem
The need to promote everything on the Home page (or at least everything that supports the current business model) often obscures these main entry points. It's hard to find "Start here" when the page is full of promos yelling "No, click me first!".
The Solution:
Make the entry points look like entry points (i.e., make the search box look like a search box, and the list of sections look like a list).
Label them clearly with labels like "Search," "Browse by Category," "Sign in," or "Start here".
2: Home Page Navigation vs. Persistent Navigation
The Question: How important is it for the navigation on the Home page to be the same as on the rest of the site?
The Answer: "Yes, it can be different. But not too different".
Given the Home page's unique responsibilities, it often makes sense not to use the standard persistent navigation there.
3: Acceptable Differences
Section descriptions: The Home page has more room, so you may want to add a descriptive phrase to each section name, or even list the subsections—something you don't have space for on other pages.
More space for identity: The Site ID on the Home page is usually larger, like the main sign over a store's entrance, and it needs space for the tagline.
Different orientation: The Home page may require a different layout, so it may use vertical navigation while the rest of the site uses horizontal (or vice-versa).
3: The Unbreakable Rule: Consistency
It is vital not to make any changes you don't have to. The Home page navigation and the persistent navigation need to have enough in common so users can recognize them immediately as two different versions of the same thing.
You MUST keep the section names exactly the same: the same order, the same wording, and the same grouping.
You MUST keep as many of the same visual cues as possible: the same typeface, colors, and capitalization.
The Cost of Failure: The Wildfire.com site is a bad example.
On the Home page, the sections are "I Want Wildfire!," "WildTalk," and "The Company."
On all other pages, the sections become "CONSUMER," "ENTERPRISE," "COMPANY," etc.
The Result: "I Want Wildfire" becomes "Consumer." "WildTalk" disappears entirely. "Enterprise" appears out of nowhere. The names aren't in the same order. It's impossible to recognize these two systems as being related. When a user leaves the Home page, they have to figure out the site's navigation all over again.
2: The Trouble with Pulldowns (Dropdown Menus)
Designers are always looking for ways to create more space on the Home page, and a common approach is using pulldown menus. They definitely save space.
Unfortunately, they suffer from several problems.
3: The 3 Problems with Pulldowns
You have to seek them out. You have to click on the pulldown to see the list. This means there's no chance for items on the list to "catch your eye" as you scan the page.
They're hard to scan. Designers have no control over the font, spacing, or formatting of the list, and there's no good way to divide the list into subgroups.
They're "twitchy." The fact that the list comes and goes so quickly makes it harder to read.
3: When to Use / When Not to Use Pulldowns
Pulldowns are most effective for: Alphabetized lists of items with known names, like countries, states, or products. There's no thought involved. If I'm looking for VCRs, I can just scroll down to the V's.
Pulldowns are much less effective for: Lists where I don't know the name of the thing I'm looking for, especially if the list isn't alphabetized or is long enough to require scrolling.
